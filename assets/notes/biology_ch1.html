1.1

What is biology and why do we study it?

Textbook sections 1.1 & 1.2
Video 1.1 · 7–9 min

Biology comes from two Greek words: bios meaning life, and logos meaning study. Biology is the scientific study of life or living things.

Key term
Biology — the scientific study of life and living organisms.

Properties shared by living things

  • Composed of one or more cells.
  • Can grow and reproduce.
  • Transmit genetic information to their offspring.
  • Need energy to carry out life's activities.
  • Maintain stable internal conditions — called homeostasis.
  • Capable of evolutionary adaptation to the environment.

Why we study biology

  • Helps us understand how living things work and interact with the environment.
  • Used to discover drugs that treat human diseases.
  • Applied in agriculture, forensics (DNA analysis), and ecology.
  • Used in fermentation to produce drinks like beer and wine.
  • Helps treat diseases such as AIDS, tuberculosis, and some cancers.
Real-world example

Penicillin, an antibiotic that saves millions of lives, comes from mould fungi. Wine, beer, bread, and cheese all depend on fermentation by yeast and bacteria — biology at work in everyday Ethiopian life.

Biology and other sciences

Biology merges with chemistry to study elements, molecules, and chemical bonding inside living organisms. Biology merges with physics to study processes like photosynthesis, which converts radiant energy into organic molecules.

1.2

The scientific method, step by step

Textbook section 1.3
Video 1.2 · 8–9 min

Biology is a science of inquiry. Biologists are always curious about why or how things happen. By asking questions and seeking science-based answers — the scientific method — they come up with new theories that explain new findings.

  1. Observation — careful observation, often of a problem, leads to a question. Observations can be direct (using your senses) or indirect (using tools like microscopes).
  2. Asking questions — the observation leads the scientist to ask an inquiry.
  3. Forming a hypothesis — a proposed, testable scientific explanation for the question.
  4. Testing the hypothesis — through experimentation. Results must be repeatable by other scientists to count as scientific knowledge.
  5. Making conclusions — deciding whether the hypothesis is supported or should be rejected based on the results.
  6. Communicating findings — publishing results so other scientists and students can learn from them.
Key term
Hypothesis — a proposed scientific explanation (possible answer) for a question. A scientific hypothesis must be testable.
Worked example from the textbook

Your torch (hand lamp) doesn't switch on. Observation: the torch is dark. Question: why won't it light up? Hypothesis: maybe the batteries are dead. Experiment: replace the batteries and test again. Conclusion: if it now works, the hypothesis was correct.

1.3

Laboratory tools every biologist uses

Textbook section 1.4.1
Video 1.3 · 9–11 min

Biologists use many types of tools in the laboratory for measuring, observing, and culturing micro-organisms. The most important is the hand lens — a convex lens in a metal loop that gives an enlarged image, though it isn't enough to see the detail inside cells.

Culturing and sterilizing equipment

Autoclave
Sterilizes samples using high heat and pressurized steam; kills microorganisms and their spores.
Incubator
Maintains the ideal temperature for growing micro-organisms.
Petri dish
A flat dish with a cover, used with gelling culture media like agar.
Culture tubes
Used to culture micro-organisms in solid or liquid media.

General-purpose lab equipment

Flask
Flat-bottomed container with a long neck, for mixing or gently heating solutions.
Balance
Accurately weighs specimens and prepares solutions.
Dropper
Controls the precise amount of liquid added to a reaction.
Tongs
Metallic, scissor-shaped tool for lifting hot objects.
Beaker
Cylindrical glass container for holding or mixing solutions.
Hotplate
Used for heating solutions or culture media.
pH meter
Measures the acidity or basicity of a substance.
Thermometer
Measures the temperature of a substance.
Forceps
Used to hold or pick up small objects.
Spatula
Spoon-shaped tool for mixing, stirring, and scraping.
Wash bottle
Flexible bottle used to rinse lab materials with adjustable pressure.
Bunsen burner
Produces an open flame for heating and sterilizing.

Dissection equipment

Dissecting tool kit
Includes a scalpel, scissors, and forceps for dissecting animals such as frogs.
Dissecting pan
The pan the specimen rests on during dissection.
Crucible
A small heat-resistant container, usually used to melt elements.
Real-world example

An autoclave sterilizes lab equipment with heat and steam — the very same principle hospitals use to sterilize surgical tools before an operation.

Field tools — studying biology outdoors

Textbook section 1.4.2
Video 1.4 · 5–7 min

Not all biology happens in a laboratory. Field tools let biologists study organisms directly in their natural habitat, rather than under controlled indoor conditions.

Insect net
A net bag of cloth or fine mesh on a wire loop and pole, used to collect insects.
Fishing net
Made from woven fibres in a grid pattern, used to catch fish or aquatic organisms.
Real-world example

Ethiopian biologists use insect nets in the field to safely collect specimens for study without harming the surrounding ecosystem.

The light microscope — parts and magnification

Textbook section 1.5.1
Video 1.5 · 9–11 min

There are two main types of microscope. A light microscope uses a beam of light to view specimens. An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons and electromagnets instead of light and glass lenses — giving much higher magnification, enough to see viruses.

Main parts of the light microscope

PartFunction
Eyepiece (ocular)The part you look through; standard magnification 10x.
Eyepiece tubeHolds the eyepiece above the objective lens.
Objective lensesMagnify the specimen further; usually 4x to 100x.
ArmConnects and supports all the parts of the microscope.
Body tubeConnects the eyepiece to the objective lenses.
Nose pieceRotating mount that holds multiple objective lenses.
Coarse adjustmentBrings the specimen into rough focus quickly.
Fine adjustmentSharpens the focus precisely.
StageWhere the specimen slide is placed.
Stage clipHolds the slide in place on the stage.
ApertureThe hole in the stage through which light passes.
IlluminatorThe microscope's light source, located at the base.
CondenserFocuses light from the illuminator onto the specimen.
Diaphragm (iris)Controls how much light reaches the specimen.
BaseThe bottom part that supports the whole microscope.
Total magnification = eyepiece × objective lens
Example: a 10x eyepiece with a 40x objective lens gives a total magnification of 400x — enough to clearly see most cells.

1.6

How to handle and use a microscope correctly

Textbook section 1.5.2
Video 1.6 · 8–10 min

Carrying and setting up

  1. Carry the microscope by gripping the arm with one hand and supporting the base with the other.
  2. Hold it upright at all times and never bump it against anything.
  3. Make sure the slide is clean and dry before placing it on the stage.
  4. Center the area of interest over the hole in the stage.

Focusing procedure

  1. Always focus on low power (4x objective) first, even if you eventually need higher magnification.
  2. Use the coarse adjustment knob first, then the fine adjustment knob to sharpen the image.
  3. To increase magnification, center the area of interest, then switch to a higher power lens.
  4. Always focus by moving the lens and specimen further apart, never closer together.

Special rules and putting it away

  • Use immersion oil only with the 100x objective, and only one drop.
  • Never touch the lens surfaces with fingers or anything else.
  • Lower the stage before removing the slide.
  • Clean the microscope with lens paper and alcohol when finished.
  • Place the 4x objective over the stage and lower the stage fully before storing.
  • Turn off the light, wrap the cord properly, and return it to its correct cabinet.

1.7

General laboratory safety rules

Textbook section 1.6
Video 1.7 · 7–9 min

Safety is the condition of being protected from harm or danger. Laboratory safety matters before doing any activity — working carefully, dealing with minor problems before they grow, and keeping safety constantly in mind.

  1. Follow instructions — read and understand the lab procedure fully before starting, and know how to use all equipment.
  2. Know your safety equipment — locate the fire extinguisher and first-aid kit, and check that they work.
  3. Dress for the laboratory — wear a lab coat, safety goggles, gloves, closed shoes, and a respirator mask for toxic chemicals.
  4. Never eat or drink in the lab — and never store food in a fridge containing experiments or chemicals.
  5. Never taste or sniff chemicals — always label containers and read the label before use.
  6. Act responsibly — never randomly mix chemicals; it can cause explosions, fire, or toxic gas.
  7. Clean up properly — start and end every session with clean, properly stored equipment and waste.
  8. Handle chemicals properly — wear a respirator mask for toxic chemicals and never let them touch bare skin.
  9. Know accident response — flood a burn or chemical exposure with cold tap water immediately.
  10. Stay vigilant — never leave containers open or unlabeled; safety and hygiene apply at all times.

Chapter 1 summary

  • Biology is the scientific study of living things.
  • Living organisms interact with themselves and the environment at all levels of biological organization.
  • Biological inquiry depends on the scientific method — a series of steps scientists follow when experimenting and reporting results.
  • The scientific method ensures conclusions are based on observation and that other scientists can repeat the experiments.
  • Biologists use many tools — for measuring, observing, and culturing — with the microscope being the most important.
  • A microscope is an instrument used to see very small objects through magnification.
  • Laboratory safety must come first in every activity, since careful work prevents minor problems from becoming major ones.
